# The Future of Work with AI

> *Everyone is asking the wrong question. "Will AI take my job?" is less useful than "How will the job change, and what do I need to be good at to do it well in five years?" This chapter is for the people who need to plan for that future — and for the organisations responsible for helping their people get there.*

## The Numbers, Honestly

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 projects that 85 million job roles will be "displaced" by automation by 2025–2030, while 97 million new roles will emerge. The net is positive. The individual experience frequently isn't — displaced people are not automatically the people who fill the new roles, and the timeline of displacement often precedes the timeline of new role creation.

Goldman Sachs estimated in 2023 that 300 million full-time jobs globally could be partially automated by current AI. They did not estimate that 300 million jobs would disappear — they estimated that many tasks within those jobs would change.

The OECD's 2025 analysis of AI's employment impact found something more nuanced than either "AI will take all jobs" or "AI will create more jobs than it destroys": the impact varies dramatically by task composition, sector, and whether the employer invests in worker adaptation. Organisations that combined AI deployment with reskilling investment saw productivity gains and employment stability. Organisations that deployed AI without worker investment saw productivity gains and employment instability.

The honest answer to "will AI replace my job?" is: the tasks within your job will change significantly. Some will be automated. New tasks — many involving AI oversight, prompt engineering, exception handling, and judgment on cases the AI can't handle — will emerge. Whether you do those new tasks or someone else does depends largely on whether you adapt.

This is not a comfortable answer. But it's a more useful one than either "don't worry, it won't affect you" or "everyone is being replaced."

## The Three Trajectories of Job Change

McKinsey's detailed occupational analysis identifies three trajectories for how AI affects different roles:

### Trajectory 1: Augmentation (Majority of Professional Roles)

The AI handles the routine, high-volume, lower-judgment tasks within a role. The human handles the complex, context-dependent, relationship-driven tasks that the AI does poorly. The human becomes significantly more productive — producing more output per hour, handling more complexity, covering more scope.

**Examples:**
- **Radiologist:** AI reads routine imaging (normal chest X-rays, standard CT scans). Radiologist focuses on complex cases, ambiguous findings, communication with clinical team, and oversight of AI's performance. Output per radiologist increases; accuracy on complex cases may improve because cognitive load on routine cases is reduced.
- **Software engineer:** AI generates boilerplate code, handles routine debugging, writes tests. Engineer focuses on system design, complex algorithm development, stakeholder requirements translation, and AI-generated code review. Coding velocity increases 40–55% (GitHub Copilot data).
- **Financial analyst:** AI pulls data, runs calculations, generates first-draft analysis, produces visualisations. Analyst focuses on interpretation, narrative, client communication, and the judgment calls that don't fit the model. Analyst covers significantly more companies or more complex analysis.

**What changes for augmented roles:**
- Time allocation shifts — more of the role is high-judgment, less is routine
- Output volume increases — you can cover more
- Quality bar rises — because AI handles the routine, human errors on routine tasks (that AI would catch) are less tolerated
- Skill mix changes — the ability to evaluate AI outputs critically, to prompt effectively, to integrate AI into workflow becomes a core professional competency

### Trajectory 2: Transformation (Some roles restructure around AI)

The role itself is redefined. The tasks that occupied most of the role are automated; a new role emerges that looks quite different from the original.

**Examples:**
- **Data entry clerk → Data quality specialist:** Routine data entry is automated. A smaller number of people oversee automated ingestion pipelines, handle exceptions, ensure data quality, and manage the governance of automated processes.
- **First-line IT support → AI operations specialist:** L1 ticket handling (password resets, known issues) is fully automated. A smaller team manages the AI systems, handles escalations the AI can't resolve, monitors quality, and continuously improves the AI's resolution capability.
- **Insurance underwriter (standard lines) → AI underwriting oversight:** Standard personal lines (auto, home) are mostly algorithmically underwritten. Human underwriters focus on complex commercial risks, exceptions to models, model governance, and novel risk categories.

**What changes for transformed roles:**
- Headcount in the original role decreases; headcount in the new role is smaller but often better-compensated
- The new role requires understanding of AI systems in addition to the domain
- Career paths must be deliberately designed — it is not obvious how a data entry clerk becomes a data quality specialist without organisational investment in that transition

### Trajectory 3: Disruption (Some roles see significant demand reduction)

The task composition of the role overlaps very heavily with AI capabilities, and the tasks the AI cannot do are a small fraction of the total. Demand for the role declines.

**Examples of roles with high disruption risk:**
- Routine translation (machine translation has reached professional quality for most language pairs)
- Basic paralegal work (document review, citation checking, discovery — all moving to AI)
- Routine content creation (commodity blog posts, product descriptions, ad copy — AI handles at scale)
- Data annotation (self-supervised learning and synthetic data are reducing the volume of human labelling required)
- Certain customer service roles (containment rates rising rapidly)

**The important caveat:** "Disruption risk" does not mean "disappears." It means demand contracts and the remaining roles require different skills. The translation market grew after machine translation because lower prices enabled more translation. The remaining translators focus on literary, legal, and highly nuanced content where machine quality is insufficient. The market is different, not absent.

## The New Skills That Actually Matter

Asking "will I be replaced?" is the wrong question. Asking "what skills are AI-proof?" is slightly better but still wrong — no skill is entirely AI-proof, and the landscape is moving fast enough that today's answer is wrong in two years. The right question is: what skills are becoming *more* valuable as AI handles more of the work?

### Meta-Cognitive Skills: Thinking About Thinking

AI is very good at pattern-matching from training data. It is not good at knowing when a pattern doesn't apply, recognising novel situations that don't fit established patterns, or adjusting its approach when its model of the world is wrong.

The humans who remain most valuable in AI-augmented environments are those who can do this — who can say "the AI's analysis is technically correct, but it's missing the contextual factor that changes everything." This requires strong domain expertise combined with the ability to critically evaluate AI outputs.

**What this means in practice:** Domain experts who invest in AI literacy — understanding what AI can and can't do well — become dramatically more valuable than domain experts who avoid AI or AI technologists without domain expertise.

### Communication and Translation

The outputs of AI systems need to be contextualised, interpreted, and communicated to people who will act on them. A fraud detection model that flags 200 transactions needs a human who can explain to a senior manager which 10 of those 200 are worth executive attention and why. A contract review AI that finds 40 clauses worth negotiating needs a lawyer who can explain to the CEO why 5 of them are actually dealbreakers.

Translation between AI capability and human decision-making is not automated. It is a premium skill.

### Judgment on Novel and Ambiguous Cases

Every AI system is defined by its training distribution — the patterns it was trained on. Novel situations, ambiguous cases, and genuine ethical dilemmas are outside the training distribution. This is where human judgment is required, and where it is most valuable.

As AI handles the clear cases, human judgment is increasingly concentrated on the hard cases — the ones with genuine ambiguity, high stakes, or significant ethical dimensions. This is not a consolation prize. It is the core of professional work, stripped of the routine that used to dilute it.

### Relationship and Trust

Customer relationships, leadership, negotiation, empathy, and the building of genuine trust between people — these are not automated by current AI and are not close to being automated. The proportion of professional work that involves these skills is increasing as AI handles the rest.

## What Organisations Must Do: The Reskilling Imperative

The World Economic Forum estimates that 50% of all employees globally will need significant reskilling by 2025. Many of those five years have elapsed, and the reskilling has not happened at scale.

This is not primarily a market failure. It is an organisational priority failure. Reskilling AI-displaced workers is expensive, slow, and uncertain — and the people making investment decisions often won't see the consequences of under-investing until it's too late to correct.

### The Skills That Matter Across All Roles

**AI literacy (everyone):** Understanding what AI systems can and can't do, how to interact with them effectively, how to evaluate their outputs critically, and when to trust vs. verify. This is the new computer literacy. The organisations that achieve universal AI literacy fastest will outperform those that treat it as optional.

**Prompt engineering (most professional roles):** The ability to communicate effectively with AI systems is becoming a professional competency with the same status as "ability to use Excel." It is learnable, it is teachable, and the gap between people who have it and people who don't is measurable in productivity.

**AI output evaluation (roles where AI output quality matters):** Domain experts need specific training in evaluating AI outputs in their domain. A lawyer evaluating AI-generated legal analysis needs to understand where LLMs hallucinate in legal contexts specifically. A clinician evaluating AI-generated differential diagnoses needs to understand where medical AI fails.

**Data literacy (increasingly everyone):** As AI systems surface more data-driven insights and recommendations, the ability to interpret, question, and appropriately weight data — rather than accepting it uncritically — becomes broadly important.

### The Investment Required

IBM's 2023 survey of 3,000 CEOs found 40% expected significant reskilling requirements in their organisations over the next three years. Only 34% believed their organisations had the right programmes in place.

The gap is an organisational failure of imagination. "We'll hire people with the right skills" is not a plan — the people with AI-native skills are in short supply and expensive. "We'll train people" is the plan, but it requires sustained investment and leadership commitment.

**A realistic training investment model for AI transition:**
- ₹25,000–₹50,000 per employee for AI literacy training (2–4 day programme)
- ₹1,00,000–₹2,50,000 per employee for role-specific AI integration training
- ₹3,00,000–₹8,00,000 per employee for deep reskilling into new AI-adjacent roles
- Management time: significant — training without manager reinforcement doesn't stick

For a 1,000-person organisation executing a serious reskilling programme, this represents ₹5–15 crore over 3 years. It is less than the cost of one avoidable AI project failure.

## What India's Workforce Faces Specifically

India's AI and workforce story has dimensions that are distinct from Western markets:

**The demographic dividend meets AI.** India's working-age population is still growing. The 65 million new workforce entrants expected by 2030 are entering a market where AI is already transforming what employers need. The IndiaAI FutureSkills programme — integrating AI curriculum into undergraduate and postgraduate education — is the policy response. Its effectiveness will determine whether India's demographic dividend becomes a demographic asset or a demographic burden.

**English proficiency as an AI advantage.** Most frontier AI models perform best in English. Indian professionals with English fluency are in a stronger position relative to non-English-speaking developing markets when using current AI tools. This is a temporary advantage — multilingual AI is rapidly improving — but it's real now.

**IT services sector exposure.** India's IT services sector — a $250+ billion employer — is highly exposed to AI disruption. Routine software development, testing, and documentation — tasks that employ hundreds of thousands — are increasingly AI-augmented. The sector's response (Infosys Topaz, TCS AI.Cloud, Wipro AI360) is to move up the value chain: AI strategy, AI implementation, AI governance. The firms themselves are navigating the transition while advising clients on theirs.

**Agriculture and informal economy.** For the majority of India's workforce in agriculture and the informal economy, the primary AI impact is not job displacement — it's agricultural AI tools (crop advisory, market price prediction, weather forecasting) and financial inclusion AI (credit access, insurance). For this workforce, AI is more likely to be an enabler than a disruptor in the medium term.

## For Enterprise Architects: Your Specific Future

The enterprise architect's role is not going away. It is changing in the ways described in [03-EA-Practice/02-AI-Design-Decisions.md](../03-EA-Practice/02-AI-Design-Decisions.md). The trajectory is augmentation — architects who understand AI become dramatically more effective; architects who don't become progressively less relevant.

The specific investments that matter for enterprise architects over the next five years:

1. **Hands-on AI building experience** — not managing AI projects, but actually building AI features, RAG pipelines, and agentic systems. You cannot architect what you haven't experienced.

2. **AI governance expertise** — NIST AI RMF, EU AI Act, DPDPA, responsible AI frameworks. This is increasingly where EA adds distinctive value: the connective tissue between AI capability and institutional accountability.

3. **AI economics literacy** — understanding token costs, model selection trade-offs, FinOps for AI. The architect who can answer "what will this AI system cost at enterprise scale?" is immediately more valuable than one who can't.

4. **The "human orchestrator" identity** — as agentic AI handles more routine architectural analysis, the architect's role shifts toward orchestrating AI systems, curating their outputs, and applying the contextual judgment that AI cannot. Embrace this shift rather than resisting it.

The enterprise architects who navigate this transition well will be more important, more visible, and more impactful in five years than they are today. The ones who don't will find their role progressively automated. The choice is active, not passive.
